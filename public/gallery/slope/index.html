Should-cost & learning-curve estimating

The hundredth unit should not cost what the first one did.

Slope prices production programmes on the learning curve rather than on last year's actuals. On an 85% slope, every doubling of cumulative units takes 15% out of unit cost — by unit 512, nine doublings in, you should be building at roughly 23% of first-unit cost.

Estimating that assumes you get better

A rate that never improves is not a forecast. It is an admission.

  • Fitted, not assumed

    The slope is regressed from your own touch-labour hours per unit, so a programme running at 92% is priced at 92% — not at the 85% an airframe textbook suggests.

  • Breaks in the curve

    A design change, a line move or a two-year production gap all reset learning, and the model shows the step rather than smoothing a break that really happened.

  • Rate-effect separated

    Cost improvement from learning is held apart from cost improvement from volume, because they unwind differently when the buy is cut.

  • Defensible to an auditor

    Every fitted curve exports with its underlying unit data and its R², so the basis of estimate survives a question about where the number came from.
