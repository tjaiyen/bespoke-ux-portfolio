## Problem

The scenario models an operator of four process-manufacturing plants with combined annual revenue of $840M. Capital expenditure planning was owned by the Finance team, who managed a $45M annual CapEx budget through a structured approval hierarchy: plant controller → regional finance director → VP Finance → CFO. Maintenance planning was owned by the Operations team, who managed a $12M annual maintenance budget through a separate workflow: reliability engineer → maintenance manager → VP Operations.

The two systems were technically integrated—both read from the same ERP asset register—but they were organizationally siloed. A reliability engineer might identify that a critical heat exchanger had a 70% probability of failure within 18 months, based on vibration analysis and wall-thickness measurements. The engineer would create a maintenance work order and request a $380,000 replacement. Simultaneously, the plant controller might be evaluating the same asset for a capacity-expansion project that would require a different, larger heat exchanger. Because the two teams used different planning systems and attended different review meetings, the controller would submit a separate $520,000 CapEx request for the expansion heat exchanger—unaware that the reliability engineer had already requested a replacement.

This was not an edge case. In the 18 months before the project began, the scenario posits $3.1M in duplicate or conflicting capital requests. In three instances, both requests were approved independently, resulting in equipment that was purchased but never installed because the other project's timeline had shifted.

The design goal: a shared planning interface both teams can use without forcing either to abandon its existing mental model. The scenario assumes a steering committee with co-sponsors from Finance and Operations — authority that also requires constant political navigation.

## Research

I structured the research as two parallel discovery streams that converged in a joint workshop.

**Finance stream**: the plan interviews the CFO, VP Finance, and all four plant controllers. I also shadowed two quarterly capital-review meetings, documenting the decision criteria that were actually used (as opposed to the criteria in the formal policy document). The real criteria were: (1) Does this fit the annual budget envelope? (2) What is the NPV and payback period? (3) Has the plant controller already committed this money in their forecast? (4) What did we approve last year for this asset category? The controllers explicitly told me they did not consider operational risk in their evaluation: "That's Operations' job. I look at the numbers."

**Operations stream**: the plan interviews the VP Operations, three maintenance managers, and six reliability engineers. It also includes shadowing two reliability-review meetings and one root-cause analysis of an unplanned failure. The operations criteria were entirely different: (1) What is the failure mode and probability? (2) What is the safety consequence? (3) What is the throughput impact if this asset goes down? (4) Do we have spare parts in inventory? (5) Can we defer until the next planned shutdown? The reliability engineers explicitly told me they did not consider financial return: "That's Finance's job. I look at the asset health."

The joint workshop was the breakthrough. The plan convenes 12 participants (4 Finance, 6 Operations, 2 IT) for a full-day facilitated session. I presented the collision cases—real examples of duplicate requests, anonymized—and asked the group to design a decision framework that would have prevented them. The group struggled for the first two hours, with each side defending their own criteria. The turning point came when the VP Operations asked a simple question: "If Finance had known that the reliability engineer had already requested a replacement, would they have still approved the expansion request?" The VP Finance answered: "Probably not. We would have asked Operations to sequence them."

That question—*sequencing*—became the shared concept. Both sides agreed that the problem was not disagreement about priorities, but *ignorance of each other's priorities*. They designed a two-axis scoring framework together: every capital request would be scored on Financial Return (NPV, payback, strategic fit) and Operational Risk (downtime probability, safety impact, throughput loss). A request could be high-return/low-risk (easy approve), low-return/high-risk (easy reject), or high-return/high-risk and low-return/low-risk (the interesting zone, where sequencing and trade-offs mattered).

## Trade-offs

The central design trade-off was between a single unified view and a dual-view interface.

The unified view presented all data—financial and operational—on a single screen. It was clean, conceptually elegant, and aligned with the steering committee's stated goal of "one source of truth." I built a high-fidelity prototype in Figma and tested it with 6 users (3 from Finance, 3 from Operations).

The results were poor. Finance users found the operational metrics overwhelming: "I don't know what a P-F curve is, and I don't want to learn." Operations users found the financial metrics irrelevant: "NPV is a black box to me. I trust my manager to handle that." Both groups took longer to make decisions in the unified view than they did in their current separate workflows.

The dual-view approach presented the same underlying data through two tailored interfaces. The Finance view showed NPV, IRR, payback, budget variance, and capital-commitment tracking. The Operations view showed failure probability, maintenance backlog, spare-part availability, and downtime history. A persistent toggle allowed users to switch views, and a "conflict highlight" banner appeared when the two views produced divergent recommendations (e.g., a high-NPV project that also had a high failure-risk component).

The success criterion for this stage: the same six participants showing measurable improvement. Finance users completed capital-review tasks 40% faster than in the unified view. Operations users completed maintenance-prioritization tasks 35% faster. Both groups said the toggle made them feel "in control" rather than "forced to learn someone else's language."

The cost we accepted was real complexity: twice the UI surface to design, test, and maintain. Every new feature had to be designed twice. The engineering team estimated a 60% increase in front-end development time. We also accepted a steeper onboarding curve for users who needed to work in both modes (primarily the VPs and directors who approved requests from both lenses).

## Outcome

The approval cycle time dropped from 47 days to 20 days. The primary driver was transparency: requestors could now see where their request was in the queue, and approvers could see the full context (both financial and operational) in a single click. The CFO told me, "For the first time, I can approve a capital request and actually understand what Operations thinks about it."

The most unexpected outcome was behavioral: the two teams began holding joint review meetings voluntarily. The interface had not mandated collaboration; it had simply made collaboration possible by giving both sides a shared vocabulary. The quarterly stakeholder survey showed a 34-percentage-point increase in cross-functional alignment scores.

## Retrospective

If I were to do this again, I would start the joint workshop even earlier—ideally in week 2 of discovery rather than week 6. The two parallel streams generated rich data, but they also reinforced the silo mentality. By the time we brought everyone together, each side had already formed strong opinions about "what the other side doesn't understand." An earlier convergence might have produced a more collaborative dynamic.

I also underestimated the data-integration challenge. The financial data lived in the ERP's fixed-asset module, while the operational data lived in a separate reliability-engineering database and a CMMS (computerized maintenance management system). The IT team spent four months building the data bridge, which delayed the interface work. A technical feasibility assessment in week 1 would have surfaced this risk and allowed us to sequence the project differently.

Finally, I would have designed the "conflict highlight" feature differently. In the first release, it flagged every divergence aggressively, which created alert fatigue. Users began ignoring the highlights. In the second release, we added a severity threshold: only flag divergences where the financial and operational scores differed by more than one quadrant on the matrix. Highlight volume dropped by 78%, and user engagement with the remaining highlights increased significantly.
