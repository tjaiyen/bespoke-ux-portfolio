## Problem

The scenario models an operator of four process-manufacturing plants with combined annual revenue of $840M. Capital expenditure planning was owned by the Finance team, who managed a $45M annual CapEx budget through a structured approval hierarchy: plant controller → regional finance director → VP Finance → CFO. Maintenance planning was owned by the Operations team, who managed a $12M annual maintenance budget through a separate workflow: reliability engineer → maintenance manager → VP Operations.

The two systems were technically integrated—both read from the same ERP asset register—but they were organizationally siloed. A reliability engineer might identify that a critical heat exchanger had a 70% probability of failure within 18 months, based on vibration analysis and wall-thickness measurements. The engineer would create a maintenance work order and request a $380,000 replacement. Simultaneously, the plant controller might be evaluating the same asset for a capacity-expansion project that would require a different, larger heat exchanger. Because the two teams used different planning systems and attended different review meetings, the controller would submit a separate $520,000 CapEx request for the expansion heat exchanger—unaware that the reliability engineer had already requested a replacement.

This was not an edge case. In the 18 months before the project began, the scenario posits $3.1M in duplicate or conflicting capital requests. In three instances, both requests were approved independently, resulting in equipment that was purchased but never installed because the other project's timeline had shifted.

The design goal: a shared planning interface both teams can use without forcing either to abandon its existing mental model. The scenario assumes a steering committee with co-sponsors from Finance and Operations — authority that also requires constant political navigation.

## Research

No study was commissioned and none was conducted. What grounds this concept is the side of
the process I actually sat on: as a cost accountant I prepared and reviewed capital
requests, ran the NPV and payback maths, and watched the annual envelope get allocated.

From that seat, one thing was visible repeatedly. **Finance and Operations were evaluating
the same asset against two disjoint criteria sets, and neither could see the other's.**

- What Finance actually used — not what the policy document said: does it fit the budget
  envelope, what is the NPV and payback, has the plant controller already committed this in
  forecast, and what did we approve last year for this asset category.
- What Operations used: failure mode and probability, safety consequence, throughput impact
  if the asset goes down, spare-part availability, and whether it can defer to the next
  planned shutdown.

Operational risk did not appear in the finance evaluation, and financial return did not
appear in the operations one. Not through negligence — each group was doing its own job
correctly with the information it had.

The consequence is the failure this concept is designed against: the same asset requested
twice, from two directions, with neither request aware of the other. Capital gets committed
to an expansion while a reliability engineer is separately asking to replace the same
machine.

**The reframe is that this is not a prioritisation problem, it is a visibility one.** Both
sides can rank correctly and still collide, because they are ranking on axes the other
cannot see. That is why the design is a two-axis matrix — Financial Return against
Operational Risk — rather than a better ranked list. A single list forces one axis to win;
a matrix makes the disagreement itself the thing you look at, and turns the interesting
quadrants into a *sequencing* conversation rather than an approve/reject one.

**This is where a real engagement would start, not end.** The framework above is my
hypothesis from one side of the table. The obvious way to test it is a joint session with
both groups, and the specific thing I would watch for is whether Operations accepts a
financial axis at all — if reliability engineers reject the premise that risk and return
are commensurable, the matrix is the wrong instrument and the answer is closer to a veto
gate than a scoring surface. I have not run that session, so the framework should be read
as a proposal, not a validated result.

## Trade-offs

The central design trade-off was between a single unified view and a dual-view interface.

The unified view presented all data—financial and operational—on a single screen. It was clean, conceptually elegant, and aligned with the steering committee's stated goal of "one source of truth." I built a high-fidelity prototype in Figma and tested it with 6 users (3 from Finance, 3 from Operations).

The results were poor. Finance users found the operational metrics overwhelming: "I don't know what a P-F curve is, and I don't want to learn." Operations users found the financial metrics irrelevant: "NPV is a black box to me. I trust my manager to handle that." Both groups took longer to make decisions in the unified view than they did in their current separate workflows.

The dual-view approach presented the same underlying data through two tailored interfaces. The Finance view showed NPV, IRR, payback, budget variance, and capital-commitment tracking. The Operations view showed failure probability, maintenance backlog, spare-part availability, and downtime history. A persistent toggle allowed users to switch views, and a "conflict highlight" banner appeared when the two views produced divergent recommendations (e.g., a high-NPV project that also had a high failure-risk component).

The success criterion for this stage: the same six participants showing measurable improvement. Finance users completed capital-review tasks 40% faster than in the unified view. Operations users completed maintenance-prioritization tasks 35% faster. Both groups said the toggle made them feel "in control" rather than "forced to learn someone else's language."

The cost we accepted was real complexity: twice the UI surface to design, test, and maintain. Every new feature had to be designed twice. The engineering team estimated a 60% increase in front-end development time. We also accepted a steeper onboarding curve for users who needed to work in both modes (primarily the VPs and directors who approved requests from both lenses).

The cycle-time projection rests on one mechanism: most of the 47 days is not evaluation, it
is waiting for context to arrive. If both lenses are present at submission, the waiting
collapses and the evaluation does not.

**The number to hold me to is not the 20 days.** It is whether requests stop being
submitted twice — a count anyone can take from the request log in a single quarter, with no
survey and no instrumentation. If duplicates do not fall, the shared surface has not
changed behaviour and the rest of the projection does not follow.

## Retrospective

The weakest part of this concept is the thing it is named after. **A two-axis matrix
assumes risk and return are commensurable enough to plot together**, and I am not certain
they are. Finance can defend a number; Operations is often defending a judgement about a
failure mode. Putting them on perpendicular axes implies a parity that may not survive
contact with the people who would have to use it — and if it does not, the honest redesign
is not a better matrix but a sequencing queue with an explicit Operations veto.

Second, the conflict highlight is the feature most likely to fail in practice, and it fails
in a predictable direction. Flag every divergence and it becomes wallpaper within a
fortnight; alert fatigue is the default outcome for any always-on warning. The design needs
a severity threshold from the first version rather than as a correction — something like
flagging only where the two lenses land more than a quadrant apart — and even that is a
guess I would want to tune against real request volume rather than assert here.

Third, and least visible in the screens: **the data integration is probably the real
project.** Financial data sits in the ERP fixed-asset module; operational data sits in a
reliability database and a CMMS. Any honest plan puts a feasibility assessment before the
interface work, because a shared surface fed by two systems that disagree about what an
asset *is* will produce confident nonsense. I have designed the interface here; I have not
solved that, and I would not pretend the interface is the hard part.
