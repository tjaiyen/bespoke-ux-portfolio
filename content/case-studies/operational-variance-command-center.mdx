## Problem

The scenario models three manufacturing plants producing approximately 12,000 work orders per month across six product lines. Every month-end, a team of four plant controllers and two cost accountants spent fourteen calendar days reconciling standard costs against actuals. The process was entirely Excel-driven: controllers exported GL transaction summaries from the ERP, pivoted them by cost center and work order, then manually cross-referenced against production reports, inventory movements, and purchase-order receipts stored in four different systems.

The core pain was not calculation accuracy—the ERP math was correct—but *traceability*. When a $47,000 unfavorable labor variance appeared in the summary, a controller had no direct path to the underlying transactions. They would open the work-order detail, then the labor-routing detail, then the time-card detail, then the supervisor override log, each in a different module with different navigation patterns. A single variance could consume three hours of investigation. During close week, controllers were working 12-hour days, and the final two days were pure fire-fighting: prioritizing which variances to investigate and which to book as "unexplained."

The design scope I set for myself: research through delivery, assuming a cross-functional squad of two front-end engineers, a data engineer, and a QA analyst. The scenario assumes executive sponsorship from Operations Finance and direct access to plant controllers as primary design partners.

## Research

No study was commissioned and none was conducted. This design rests on something I can
defend more directly: I spent years inside month-end close as a cost accountant in
manufacturing, and the person opening the work-order detail, then the labour-routing
detail, then the time-card detail, then the supervisor override log, was me.

That is where the central observation comes from. **Controllers do not reason in
dashboards. They reason in trails.** The vocabulary is detective vocabulary — following
the breadcrumbs, peeling the onion — and it is not decoration. At 7pm on day nine of close,
what you need is not a better summary of the $47,000 you already know about; it is the next
link in the chain that explains it. The ERP hands you the aggregate and makes you rebuild
the path by hand, every time, through modules that each navigate differently.

That reframes the obvious brief. "A better dashboard with more visualisations" is the wrong
shape for this problem — it improves the view you already have instead of the traversal you
actually perform. So the concept is organised as a *variance detective*: a path from
aggregate variance down to root-cause transaction, rather than a reporting surface.

**The information architecture is a hypothesis, not a finding.** The four categories the
interface is built on — *Work Order Trail*, *Supplier Change*, *Routing Deviation*,
*Inventory Adjustment* — are how I sorted variances in practice. They are not a taxonomy
validated against a population. A commissioned version of this project would establish that
with a card sort across the full controller group, and it is the first thing I would run.

**What would falsify the premise:** controllers given a working drill-down path who still
prefer the aggregate summary. If the trail is not the unit of work, the information
architecture is wrong, and the right answer is the dashboard the brief originally asked for.

One assumption does real work in the design, so it should be named as an assumption rather
than presented as data: variance value is heavily skewed — most variances small, a small
minority carrying most of the dollars. That is the shape I consistently saw, and it is why
the interface surfaces high-impact variances first while always preserving the path into
the long tail, since small variances that repeat every month are often the earliest signal
of a systemic problem. That distribution is modelled from experience, not measured from a
dataset I hold.

## Trade-offs

The most consequential trade-off was architectural: ERP module versus overlay.

The ERP module path offered native real-time data, single sign-on, and long-term IT ownership. The cost was an 18-month timeline, a $2.4M budget, and a user interface constrained by the ERP vendor's UI framework—which I had already evaluated and found inadequate for the breadcrumb-navigation pattern the research demanded.

The overlay path used nightly ETL to replicate ERP transaction tables into a PostgreSQL warehouse, then exposed a React-based exploration interface. The cost was 4-6 hours of data latency, a separate codebase to maintain, and a monthly reconciliation audit to catch ETL drift.

I chose the overlay for three reasons:

1. **User rhythm**: Controllers reviewed variances weekly during the month and daily only during close week. A 4-hour delay was irrelevant to their workflow.
2. **Design velocity**: The overlay would let a squad ship an MVP in 10 weeks and iterate with real controllers using production data. The ERP module would have required 6 months before any user saw a screen.
3. **Organizational fit**: The scenario's IT organisation is optimized for ERP maintenance, not product iteration. The overlay squad reported to Operations Finance, not IT, which aligned decision-making with user outcomes.

The cost is real, and it is the failure mode I designed against. A separate pipeline invites a rounding-difference mismatch between the ERP's decimal precision and the warehouse default — the kind that does not announce itself, but accumulates into a drift in WIP valuation and surfaces months later as an unexplained few-hundred-dollar gap in one plant. That is expensive precisely because it erodes trust in the tool at the moment it should be earning it. The design answer is an automated monthly reconciliation that compares ETL totals against ERP totals and flags any divergence over $100, so the drift is caught by the system rather than by a controller. The point of the guard is that it fails loudly and early; a silent pipeline is the one I would not trust.

## Outcome

This concept was not built for production, so the outcome below is modelled, not measured. The model: with the variance trace collapsed from a multi-module hunt into a single drill-down, the investigation step leaves the critical path of the close. Applied to the fourteen-day cycle I worked, that projects to roughly three days for the same four-controller team. The number to hold me to is not the three — it is whether time-to-resolution per variance falls at all, which is what I would instrument first.

The outcome I would most want to be true is a change in *when* the tool is used, not how
fast it runs. If variance investigation stops being a close-week fire drill and becomes
something a controller does on a Tuesday because the cost of looking has dropped to near
zero, the design has changed the work rather than accelerated it. That is a behavioural
claim about an unbuilt tool, so it is a hypothesis — but it is the one I would measure,
and it is measurable: the ratio of mid-month to close-week sessions.

## Retrospective

The thing I am least sure about is whether the trail metaphor survives contact with a
controller who does not share it. It is the organising idea of the whole interface, it
comes from one person's experience of the job — mine — and a mental model that is
idiosyncratic rather than general would produce an information architecture that feels
wrong to everyone else. That is the first thing a real engagement should attack, not
confirm.

Second: **export is not a feature here, it is the exit.** Controllers live in Excel and
will want variance data in their own models for ad-hoc work. A minimal CSV dump does not
serve that; the export needs to carry the same breadcrumb paths the interface uses, or the
context that made the number meaningful is stripped on the way out. Designing it last is
how tools end up with an export nobody uses.

Third, and easy to underrate from the screens: **the pipeline is where the trust is won or
lost.** A separate ETL invites a rounding-difference drift between the ERP's precision and
the warehouse default — the kind that accumulates quietly in WIP valuation and surfaces
months later as an unexplained gap in one plant. An automated monthly reconciliation that
fails loudly on any divergence is not a nice-to-have; without it, the first silent
mismatch costs more credibility than the tool ever earned.

Finally, a political dimension I would plan for rather than discover: taking variance
investigation *out* of the ERP reads to some finance stakeholders as a critique of the ERP
investment. The framing that defuses it is that the overlay makes the ERP's data more
usable rather than replacing it — and that argument lands far better before the build than
after.
