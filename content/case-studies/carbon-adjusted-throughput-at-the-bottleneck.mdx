## Problem

Theory-of-Constraints throughput accounting has one job: rank a plant's product mix by
dollar contribution per unit of its scarcest resource, so the mix decision optimizes for
whatever is actually the bottleneck rather than for volume or margin percentage. It is a
sixty-year-old idea and it works. What it has never had to answer is whether the product a
dollar-only ranking favors is also, at that exact same bottleneck, disproportionately
carbon-intensive — a genuinely different question layered onto an established one, not a
replacement for it.

The pilot computes two independent rankings against one bottleneck — an individually-quick-
frozen tunnel's own throughput minutes, the classic physical constraint in frozen-vegetable
processing. Dollar-throughput per constraint-minute, descending, is the standard
Theory-of-Constraints priority, unchanged. Carbon per constraint-minute, ascending, is the
new signal. A product is flagged only when its carbon rank trails its dollar rank by more
than a set threshold — the shape of a real tension, not a symmetric diff between two
numbers.

## What the pilot proves and what it explicitly does not

The engine's own docstring is unusually disciplined about the difference, and the design
here follows it rather than smoothing it over. Three limits are named directly in the
source, not added for this study:

**The constraint itself is assumed, not verified.** A real plant would need to confirm the
freezer tunnel actually is its bottleneck — the first of Theory of Constraints' own
"five focusing steps" — before this ranking means anything. No capacity-utilization study
backing that assumption exists anywhere in the engine's source research; it is stated as an
illustrative choice, one resource only, not a claim about any real plant's actual
constraint.

**The carbon input carries a stronger caveat than any other figure in the engine.**
Sales price and variable cost are ordinary sample data. Carbon intensity per pound has no
real product-level study, no GHG Protocol Scope 1–3 calculation, and no emissions-factor
lookup behind it anywhere in the source research — it illustrates the mechanic the tool
computes, not a number to act on. Layered on top of that: Scope 3 estimation error commonly
runs ±30% or more, which can swamp a constraint-minute-level signal entirely, and the tool
has no way to detect that from its own output — only a real measurement program could.

**The flagging threshold is a stated guess, not a derived policy.** How many rank positions
of divergence should trigger a flag is a module constant the code keeps deliberately local
rather than promoting into the engine's shared configuration registry — because that
registry's own governance rule requires a real, dated origin for a threshold, and this one
has none to give honestly. A different plant could reasonably want it tighter or looser.

## Outcome

Nothing here was deployed against a real plant, so there is no outcome to report — the
pilot's own test suite, fifteen cases re-run directly for this study, covers the ranking
arithmetic and the one-directional flag logic, not a claim about real product mix. The
honest claim is narrower than a platform feature: this is the first real test of whether
the signal is even worth building further, framed as a go/no-go pilot because a single
search pass found no prior art to validate the approach against — not because the idea is
untested elsewhere and unusually novel here.

**What would falsify this:** if a real capacity study showed the freezer tunnel is not
actually the plant's constraint, every ranking in this pilot is answering a question about
the wrong resource, and the concept would need to restart from identifying the real
bottleneck before the carbon signal means anything at all.

## Where this would break

**Scope 3 error could make the whole comparison noise, not signal.** A ±30% band on the
input feeding a constraint-minute-level ranking is large enough to flip a product's carbon
rank on its own, before any real change in the plant's operations. This pilot has no
internal way to tell a real divergence from measurement error — that check has to come from
outside it, from an actual emissions-measurement program, and until one exists the ranking
should be read as directional at best.

**A single-pass prior-art search is a weak claim to build a "white space" argument on.** It
is stronger than nothing and weaker than a systematic citation trace across the accounting
and sustainability literature. If a real methodology combining these two signals exists and
the search simply missed it, the honest framing changes from "no one has built this" to
"we didn't find who has" — a materially different claim, and one this pilot cannot
distinguish from its own output.
