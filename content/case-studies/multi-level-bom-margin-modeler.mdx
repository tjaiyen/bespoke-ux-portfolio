## Problem

The scenario models a company manufacturing industrial pumps and valves in a configure-to-order model. Each product family shared a common platform BOM with 8,000-15,000 line items, but individual customer orders could specify hundreds of variations: different seal materials, motor sizes, flange ratings, and control options. When a supplier increased the price of a stainless-steel casting by 12%, or when engineering introduced a new sub-assembly design, product managers needed to understand the margin impact across the entire product family.

The existing process was analyst-dependent. A product manager would submit a request ticket to the cost accounting team. An analyst would then spend 3-5 days building a custom Excel model: exporting the indented BOM from the ERP, flattening the multi-level structure into a cost-rollup table, applying the proposed change, and calculating the new standard cost for each affected finished good. The analyst would then prepare a summary slide deck. By the time the product manager received the answer, the supplier negotiation window might have closed, or the engineering change request might have already been approved on intuition rather than data.

The design goal: an interactive tool that lets product managers explore margin impacts themselves, without analyst intervention, while preserving the analytical rigor of the existing Excel models. The scenario assumes a delivery squad of two full-stack engineers and a data architect, sponsored by Product Management.

## Why the question never gets asked

No study was commissioned and none was conducted. I was on the receiving end of this
workflow rather than observing it: as a cost accountant I built the standard-cost rollups
and I was the analyst queue. The "what-if" requests landed on me.

That vantage point makes one thing plain. **The cost is not the calculation — it is the
round trip.** The maths in a BOM rollup is not hard; what is expensive is that the person
with the question cannot ask it directly. Three separate delays stack up: formulating a
question precisely enough to hand over, waiting in the queue, then translating a
spreadsheet back into a decision for someone else.

The second observation is the one the interface is actually built on, and it comes from the
mismatch I kept having to bridge by hand. **The requester and the bill of materials use
different vocabularies.** Nobody asks about "BOM level 7, component 8847-B." They ask about
the seal kit, the motor assembly, the control module — *functional* groupings that the
structural hierarchy does not encode. Every request required me to translate a functional
question into a structural traversal, and that translation is precisely the work a tool
should absorb.

So the design lets someone enter through a functional view and drill into the structural
BOM, rather than navigating the hierarchy from the top down. **The functional groupings
shown — Fluid Contact, Power Train, Control System, Structural Housing — are illustrative
of that idea, not a validated taxonomy.** In a real engagement they would come from a card
sort with the people making the requests, because the grouping is domain-specific and
almost certainly differs per product line.

**The strongest evidence I have is second-hand from my own inbox**, and it is worth stating
as such: the requests that actually got submitted were a fraction of the questions people
wanted answered. Most variations died at the formulation step because the round trip was
not worth it for a maybe. That is the behaviour the design targets — the unasked question,
not the queued one. It is also the thing I would instrument first, because if
self-service does not increase the *number* of questions asked, the tool has only made an
existing workflow faster rather than changing what people can do.

## 3D or breadcrumbs

The most visible trade-off was between a 3D interactive BOM visualization and a focused 2D breadcrumb-and-cascade interface.

The 3D visualization was developed as a proof-of-concept in Three.js. It rendered the BOM as a collapsible tree of nodes in 3D space, with color coding for margin impact and animated transitions for "exploding" assemblies. It is the kind of artefact that wins a room — which is precisely why it needed a performance bar set before anyone fell in love with it. It is also the kind of artefact that acquires executive sponsorship before anyone has asked whether it works.

But user testing told a different story. The evaluation plan calls for task-based testing with five product managers, answering three questions against the 3D prototype: (1) "If this bearing price increases by 10%, which finished goods are affected and by how much?" (2) "What is the total margin impact across the product family?" (3) "Which alternative component would minimize the margin hit?" The threshold I set for the 3D direction: at least 4 of 5 participants completing all three tasks within 10 minutes. My expectation was that it would fail that bar — which is why the plan tests it before committing engineering to it. The others got lost in the 3D navigation, missed quantitative details that were hidden in tooltips, and expressed frustration: "It looks cool, but I can't do my job with it."

The 2D alternative used a horizontal breadcrumb trail for BOM navigation (Platform → Product Line → Assembly → Sub-Assembly → Component) and a synchronized table below showing the margin cascade. Changing a component's cost or quantity updated the table in real time, with color-coded cells showing positive/negative margin impact. The success criterion for the alternative: all five participants completing the same three tasks in under four minutes.

I discard the 3D direction and accept the perception cost that comes with it. The accepted cost was real: the visible, demo-friendly artefact disappears, and the project's success metrics have to be reframed around user outcomes rather than visual innovation. The benefit was a 10-week MVP timeline instead of a 24-week timeline, and a user-tested interaction model that product managers could adopt immediately.

## Outcome

This concept was not built and never shipped, so the figures below are projections from the
model rather than measurements. The model rests on one substitution: replace a 3-5 day
round trip with a direct manipulation, and the questions that were never worth asking
become askable. Everything downstream — analyst time freed, self-service rate — follows
from that substitution holding, which is exactly the assumption to test first.

The application I find most interesting is supplier negotiation, and it is the one that
makes the design worth building rather than merely faster. A price increase on one
component is not obviously good or bad — it depends on what it removes elsewhere. A casting
that costs more but eliminates a secondary machining operation can improve total margin,
and that is invisible when you are looking at the component in isolation, which is exactly
how a line-item negotiation works.

**That is a claim about the design, not a story about a negotiation.** I have not watched
anyone take this into a supplier meeting. What I can say is that the calculation is
currently possible only by asking an analyst, and that nobody asks it during a live
negotiation.

## What I would defend least

The part I would defend least is the functional taxonomy. **Fluid Contact, Power Train,
Control System, Structural Housing are plausible groupings, not established ones**, and the
whole navigation model rests on them being right. They are almost certainly product-line
specific — a pump family and a control-systems family would not share a grouping — which
means the real design problem is not choosing a taxonomy but letting an organisation define
and maintain its own. I designed the easy version of that.

The 3D visualisation is the decision I am most confident about and it took the longest to
reach. It demos beautifully and it obscures the one thing the user needs, which is *where
am I in the hierarchy*. The lesson I would carry forward is that spatial impressiveness and
positional clarity pull against each other, and for a tool people use under time pressure,
clarity wins every time. The cost of learning that was three weeks of prototype.

Two things I underweighted. **Data quality is likely the real project** — BOM hierarchies
accumulate duplicate component codes, inconsistent units of measure and phantom levels that
carry no cost, and an interface that renders them faithfully renders nonsense confidently.
And **the analyst does not disappear**, they move to sign-off; scenarios generated by a
product manager still need review for anything that reaches a supplier or a forecast. A PDF
summary does not serve that. The export needs to carry the underlying numbers and their
provenance, and designing it last would be a mistake I have now written down instead of
made.
