## Problem

The scenario models a company manufacturing industrial pumps and valves in a configure-to-order model. Each product family shared a common platform BOM with 8,000-15,000 line items, but individual customer orders could specify hundreds of variations: different seal materials, motor sizes, flange ratings, and control options. When a supplier increased the price of a stainless-steel casting by 12%, or when engineering introduced a new sub-assembly design, product managers needed to understand the margin impact across the entire product family.

The existing process was analyst-dependent. A product manager would submit a request ticket to the cost accounting team. An analyst would then spend 3-5 days building a custom Excel model: exporting the indented BOM from the ERP, flattening the multi-level structure into a cost-rollup table, applying the proposed change, and calculating the new standard cost for each affected finished good. The analyst would then prepare a summary slide deck. By the time the product manager received the answer, the supplier negotiation window might have closed, or the engineering change request might have already been approved on intuition rather than data.

My role was to design an interactive tool that let product managers explore margin impacts themselves, without analyst intervention, while preserving the analytical rigor of the existing Excel models. I worked with a squad of two full-stack engineers and a data architect, reporting to the Director of Product Management.

## Research

The research phase began with a frustration audit: I asked 8 product managers to walk me through their last three "what-if" requests, narrating the full timeline from initial question to final answer. The stories were remarkably consistent. Every product manager described the same three-phase pain: *formulation* (struggling to express the question precisely), *waiting* (the 3-5 day analyst queue), and *translation* (converting the analyst's Excel output into a decision narrative for their own stakeholders).

The formulation phase was particularly revealing. Product managers did not think in terms of "BOM level 7, component 8847-B." They thought in terms of *functional groups*: "the motor assembly," "the seal kit," "the control module." One product manager told me, "I know that if I change the seal material, it affects everything in the corrosive-fluid product line. But the BOM doesn't know what 'corrosive-fluid product line' means."

The research plan calls for a card sort with the same eight product managers, using 50 representative component changes from a trailing year. I asked them to group the changes by "how I would investigate this." The resulting clusters were functional, not structural: *Fluid Contact*, *Power Train*, *Control System*, *Structural Housing*. This insight became a key design element: the interface would allow product managers to start from a functional view and drill into the structural BOM, rather than forcing them to navigate the hierarchy from the top down.

I also conducted cognitive walkthroughs of four existing analyst-built Excel models. Each model was a masterpiece of spreadsheet engineering—nested INDEX-MATCH formulas, conditional formatting, scenario tabs—but they shared a fatal flaw: they were *static snapshots*. If a product manager wanted to test a slightly different scenario ("what if the price increase is 8% instead of 12%?"), they had to request a new model. The average product manager generated 4.7 scenario variations per initial request, but only 1.2 were formally submitted because of the analyst queue.

## Trade-offs

The most visible trade-off was between a 3D interactive BOM visualization and a focused 2D breadcrumb-and-cascade interface.

The 3D visualization was developed as a proof-of-concept in Three.js. It rendered the BOM as a collapsible tree of nodes in 3D space, with color coding for margin impact and animated transitions for "exploding" assemblies. In stakeholder demos, it received enthusiastic applause. The Director of Digital Transformation called it "exactly the kind of innovation we need to show the board."

But user testing told a different story. The evaluation plan calls for task-based testing with five product managers, answering three questions against the 3D prototype: (1) "If this bearing price increases by 10%, which finished goods are affected and by how much?" (2) "What is the total margin impact across the product family?" (3) "Which alternative component would minimize the margin hit?" The threshold I set for the 3D direction: at least 4 of 5 participants completing all three tasks within 10 minutes. My expectation was that it would fail that bar — which is why the plan tests it before committing engineering to it. The others got lost in the 3D navigation, missed quantitative details that were hidden in tooltips, and expressed frustration: "It looks cool, but I can't do my job with it."

The 2D alternative used a horizontal breadcrumb trail for BOM navigation (Platform → Product Line → Assembly → Sub-Assembly → Component) and a synchronized table below showing the margin cascade. Changing a component's cost or quantity updated the table in real time, with color-coded cells showing positive/negative margin impact. The success criterion for the alternative: all five participants completing the same three tasks in under four minutes.

We discarded the 3D visualization and accepted the stakeholder perception cost. The accepted cost was real: the Director of Digital Transformation withdrew his support for a board demo, and we had to reframe the project's success metrics around user outcomes rather than visual innovation. The benefit was a 10-week MVP timeline instead of a 24-week timeline, and a user-tested interaction model that product managers could adopt immediately.

## Outcome

The BOM margin modeler launched in September 2023. Within the first quarter, product managers self-served 87% of what-if requests that previously went to the analyst queue. The three cost analysts were redeployed to strategic projects: standard-cost updates, make-vs-buy analysis, and long-range margin planning.

The most significant business impact was on supplier negotiations. Product managers could now walk into a negotiation with a precise understanding of the margin impact across the entire product family, not just the single component being discussed. One product manager used the tool to discover that a proposed 15% price increase on a casting would actually improve total margin by 2.3% because it eliminated a secondary machining operation. She negotiated a 12% increase instead, saving the supplier relationship while still capturing the operational benefit.

## Retrospective

In retrospect, I should have killed the 3D visualization two weeks earlier. The proof-of-concept took three weeks to build, and I knew after the first user test that it was not viable. I let it live because of stakeholder enthusiasm, which was a mistake. The time could have been spent refining the breadcrumb navigation pattern and building the functional-group taxonomy that product managers actually needed.

I also underestimated the data-quality work required. The BOM data in the ERP had inconsistencies: duplicate component codes, inconsistent unit-of-measure conversions, and phantom-level assemblies that existed in the hierarchy but carried no cost. The data architect spent six weeks cleaning and normalizing the data before the interface could render accurately. If I had run a data-quality audit during the research phase, I would have built that timeline into the project plan and set more realistic stakeholder expectations.

Finally, I wish I had designed the export functionality with the analyst workflow in mind from day one. Analysts still needed to review and sign off on product-manager-generated scenarios for audit purposes. The initial export was a simple PDF summary, but analysts needed the underlying data in a format they could validate. A structured JSON export with full provenance metadata would have streamlined the approval workflow significantly.
